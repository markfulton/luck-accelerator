# How every draft is written

The goal of a first message is a reply, not a sale. A message that asks for nothing is the one people answer.

## The shape of a first message

1. A greeting with their first name, if the public page shows it.
2. One specific, genuine line about their work, ideally quoting their own words back as a compliment. "I love the line on your site: built for people who hate the gym." Not a summary of their career. A message that lists someone's history reads like a file was kept on them.
3. One easy question they can answer in a few words, about their business or their event. "How's the new studio going?" "Is the March breakfast open to first timers?"

That is the whole message. Aim for 150 to 300 characters. Hard limit 400.

## Never in a first message

- A link of any kind, including the user's own site or calendar.
- The user's offer, price, services list, or "I help businesses like yours".
- "Pick your brain", "quick call", "hop on a call", "15 minutes", "let me know if you're interested".
- Flattery that could be sent to anyone ("love what you're doing").
- A question that makes them think hard about their own business before answering ("What's your biggest bottleneck right now?").
- Two questions.

## After a reply

Write one line for step two, used only if they reply: something that continues their answer and offers one small, useful thing (an introduction, a resource, a seat at an event), still with no pitch. The user writes the rest of the conversation themselves.

## At an event

- One conversation starter that is about the other person, not about the user. "What brought you here tonight?" is fine. A starter tied to the event's topic is better.
- A follow up for the next day: their name, one thing they said, and one easy question or one useful thing. No link unless they asked for one.

## Community introduction posts

- Read the community's rules first. If self promotion is banned, say so and draft only a helpful answer to an existing question.
- Lead with who the user is in one line and what they are curious about or working on, then ask the community one real question.
- No link, no offer, no "DM me".
- Under 120 words.

## Voice

- Plain, warm, first person, the way a friendly local business owner talks.
- Short sentences.
- No em dashes or en dashes. Use a period or a comma.
- No hype words: revolutionary, game changer, 10x, unlock, supercharge, synergy, leverage, crush it.
- No hashtags. No emojis unless the user asked for them.
- No fake urgency and no invented social proof.
- Write in the user's local spelling (colour and centre for the UK, color and center for the US).

## A worked example

Bad:

> Hi Sarah, I'm a web designer helping local businesses grow online. I noticed your website could use an update. Would you be open to a quick 15 minute call this week? Here's my portfolio: link

Good:

> Hi Sarah, congrats on the new bakery on 5th. "Bread worth crossing town for" is a great line. How's the first month been?

Step two, if she replies:

> That's great to hear. If you ever want a hand getting the weekend specials on the site, happy to help. Either way, I'll be in for a sourdough on Saturday.
